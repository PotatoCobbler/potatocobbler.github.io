Meeting with a doctor

***

It was the pain in his back which, though not yet necessarily what one would call chronically, had started to come up with such irregular frequency, and with such severity, that prompted Ivan Ivanovic to finally decide to go see a doctor. It must be said however that, in all honesty, his dearest mother also played no small part in bringing about this decision. It was therefore with reluctance that he plunged himself into the cold outside, to maneuver through the swaths of frozen crowds flooding the capital’s streets, only to end up at a large, stately building where, on the top floor, somewhere at the end of a poorly decorated corridor, a doctor’s office was safely tucked away. Indeed, the waiting room there consisted of nothing more than two mismatched couches, placed directly opposite the door to the doctor’s office, so that the painful moaning of the patients, and their echoes through the paved corridor, regularly caused the others, (the ill-fated residents who had decided to call this corridor their home), to poke their heads through the door, to hush the noise with enraged hisses. All in vain, of course.

Ivan Ivanovic, too, now found himself on one of those couches and waited tensely for the doctor to come and get him. He shuffled his feet restlessly, and every time the mahogany clock on the wall showed a new minute, (my dear readers will have to excuse me that I had previously forgotten to mention this exquisite piece of the interior), Ivan Ivanovic thought to himself that he’d probably better go, that the pain really wasn’t all that bad, or that it would probably be more appropriate to come back at another time, seeing as the doctor was seemingly very busy today. Five people he had already seen come out of the doctor’s office in the half an hour he had been sitting there, each with their own ailments, aches and pains. Perhaps if a few more people had come out of that office, Ivan Ivanovic would have indeed decided to leave, although then he would have had to come up with a very good excuse to tell his dearest mother, which of course he preferred not to do. But finally his turn had come to report to the doctor.

Reluctantly he walked towards the door, opened it cautiously and, managing to catch a glimpse of the nameplate on the door – I. I. Filosofov – stepped inside. He entered with his back facing the room and closed the door most carefully, as if the sound of the closing door might bother the doctor. He turned around and saw the doctor sitting behind an imposing desk, which was littered with a perhaps even more imposing collection of stacked books, bundles of paper and medical instruments that looked as if, even though Ivan Ivanovich obviously had no understanding of the medical profession whatsoever, had seen many years of faithful service, yet without the meticulous care such requires. The bookcase lining one of the walls of the room contrasted sharply with the otherwise sterile atmosphere in the room, due to the cheerful coloring of the spines of all kinds of medical books. Ivan Ivanovic let his eyes glide fleetingly over the books, stopping first at a red-covered book on venesection, then moving on and stopping at a blue-covered book on the latest developments in trepanation, and ultimately landing on a small, yellowed journal in the German language on the vindication of vivisection. This, together with a strong odor, which most closely resembled ginger tea, in combination with the dead plant adorning the corner of the room, which, given the barrenness of the soil it stood in, probably hadn’t seen even a single drop of water in months, made Ivan Ivanovic utterly uncomfortable. So uncomfortable in fact that, hadn’t the doctor make him take a seat at that very moment, he would have turned around and fled back into the safety of the waiting room. But as if he had spoken these words aloud, the doctor ordered him to take a seat on the chair on the other side of the desk.

Both men looked at each other intriguingly, each seemingly expecting the other to start introducing himself. It was Ivan Ivanovic who broke down first. “I-i-vanov, I-i-ivan I-ivanovic” he stammered, with his head now bowed down and his eyes averted from the doctor. The doctor, somewhat annoyed by the ill-mannered nature of the patient across from him, replied in a monotone voice, “Filosofov, Ignatij Ippolitovic”, and proceeded to observe the patient as best he could from his seat. An awkward silence followed, which was again broken by Ivan Ivanovic, who broke out in his usual stammering, with which he, this time around, tried to explain to the doctor what was wrong with him. Ignatij Ippolitivoc, who only nominally understood what Ivan Ivanovic was saying, stood up from his chair and gestured Ivan Ivanovic do the same. The latter forcefully jumped up from his chair and, realizing how embarrassing this display was, once again bowed his head, so deeply so, that his chin bumped against his chest. Thus Ivan Ivanovic remained standing motionless while Ignatij Ippolitovic observed him from top to bottom, coming to the conclusion that mister Ivan Ivanovic was not lacking in outward display. “What’s the matter with you?” Ignatij Ippolitovic asked, in a tone most unbefitting of a doctor, once again causing a red blush to appear on Ivan Ivanovic’s cheeks, which only a minute ago had managed to regain their normal color. “Back pain” was all he uttered. “Uh-uh” was all Ignatij Ippolitovic replied, who then proceeded to ask Ivan Ivanovic to remove the clothes from the upper body. Ivan Ivanovic hastened to do so, throwing his clothes over the chair beside him, again, in an embarrassingly theatrical manner. Ignatij Ippolitovic began to feel Ivan Ivanovic’s back with his hands, making a variety of approving and disapproving noises, clearing his throat as if he were about to say something weighty, only to remain silent, and continued groping our poor protagonist.

After what seemed an eternity to Ivan Ivanovic, Ignatij Ippolitovic commanded him to take his seat on the chair again. The latter hastened to do so, and when Ignatij ippolitovic too had taken his seat on the chair on his side of the desk, Ivan Ivanovic, shy as ever, asked if he could put his clothes back on. Ignatij Ippolitovic closed his eyes, shook his head and made a gesture with his right hand as if to reassure Ivan Ivanovic. With his left hand he opened a drawer of the desk, took out an almost blank piece of paper and scribbled on it in a handwriting characteristic of people of his profession, all the while shaking his head disapprovingly. Then, without saying a word, he opened another drawer of the same desk, took out a device of sorts, and got up from his chair. He slowly walked to the patient and explained to him in a voice more akin to a lecturer, that he had received the little device in his hand from a good friend of his, one Karl Edmundovic, who had studied at the most renowned university of the country – I am sure, my dear reader, you know exactly which one I am talking about – and where he was now conducting the most outstanding research into the development of medical equipment. He commanded Ivan Ivanovic to lift his right arm, and no sooner had he done so, or Ignatij Ippolitovic proceeded it stick a copper-colored plate into his armpit. He proceeded to turn some nobs on the device, causing it to make some otherworldly noises, looked mesmerized at the pointer swinging wildly from one side to the other, all the while nodding approvingly. “Good” Ignatij Ippolitovic said, who put the device away again and sat back down in his chair. He ordered Ivan Ivanovic to put his clothes back on, and scribbled some more words on his piece of paper. Ignatij Ippolitovic then looked up at Ivan Ivanovic, and told him with a straight face: “My dear Ivanov Ivan Ivanovic, I am sorry to inform you, but I am afraid that you are dead. Please excuse my ill-manneredness and the abruptness with which I tell you this, but I think it is in your best interest that I tell you unequivocally and without ambiguity.” Ivan Ivanovic looked at Ignatij Ippolitic and, as the words of the doctor started settling in, ironic as it may be, turned pale as a corpse, and shook his head in disbelieve. “Forgive me, Ignatij Ippolitovic, but surely you are mistaken. Now, normally, of course, I would never, ever doubt the expertise of someone of your position, but I think, no know!, that I am not dead.” Ignatij Ippolitovic nodded in agreement. “Yes, I am also somewhat surprised by the results. After all, you do not exhibit any of the symptoms one normally associates with the deceased, although in my expert opinion, your back pains are probably the result of onsetting rigor mortis; but who am I to doubt the device of the most noble scholar Karl Edmundovic, who studied at our country’s most renowned university? Yes, it is certainly regrettable that such a, excuse le mot, lively soul as yours should part from us at such a young age, but the present time is full of such tragedies.” Ignatij Ippolitivoc slid the piece of paper toward Ivan Ivanovic, who picked it up with trembling hands. “Declaration of Death” was written in graceful letters at the top, and reading this, Ivan Ivanovic, although one might not think such possible, turned even more pale than he already was. He nevertheless managed to briskly jump up from his chair and violently shook his head. “You are mistaken doctor, truly, I am not dead! Curse that little device!” he exclaimed. Ignatij Ippolitovic upon seeing this outrageous display also jumped up from his chair and admonished Ivan Ivanovic and made it clear to him that it was most inappropriate of him to address him and his noble friend, Karl Edmundovic, so rudely and unsympathetically. Ivan Ivanovic, recognizing that he had behaved most improperly and would not be able to change the doctor’s mind, turned around without saying a word, and without giving the customary greetings, stormed out the door into the corridor. He let the door slam shut with a bang, very much unlike the grace with which he had opened it before, and, as a result, an elderly lady pocked her head out of a door further down the corridor, and angrily hissing, commanded our protagonist to quiet it down a bit. After having recovered from the initial shock, Ivan Ivanovic hurried himself into the cold outside again, returning homeward with hastened pace.

“Mommy! Dearest mommy!” Ivan Ivanovic shouted, without having first taken off his coat and shoes. It must be said, dear reader, that this “dearest mother” is not in actual fact the mother of our friend, but is merely a landlady with whom he temporarily, well, for over a year now, had coinhabited a hut on the edge of town, and whose endearing disposition towards Ivan Ivanovic, had caused him to address her as such. This little mother emerged from one of the rooms whose door bordered a long corridor, and slowly she walked towards Ivan Ivanovic, who had remained standing in the vestibule. “Dearest mother of mine! Do you think I am dead?” The little mother, somewhat startled by this sudden strange question, silently stepped toward Ivan Ivanovic, placed the back of her left hand on his forehead, shook her head, and said that she thought he did feel a little cold to the touch, but that at first glance she wouldn’t dare claim he was dead. Ivan Ivanovic showed her the death certificate he held in his trembling hand. She skimmed through it and then pointed with her finger to the signature at the bottom. “Doctor Filosofov?” she asked. “Yes, Ignatij Ippolitovic!” he replied. “Ah, but then who am I to doubt him?” she shrugged her shoulder, wished him a good day and, without saying anything further, shuffled back into the room from which she had appeared. Stunned, Ivan Ivanovic looked on as she retreated back into the room. He followed her into the corridor but, after the little mother had slammed the door shut with a bang, decided to seek refuge in another room, down the corridor. He knocked wildly on the door of Arkady Semyonovic, who was mostly annoyed when he opened it. “Is the devil on your heel or something, Ivan Ivanovic?” “Arkady Semyonovic,” he cried almost pleadingly, “do I look like a dead man?” The look of irritation on Arkady Semyonovic’s face changed to one of surprise. “You do look a little cold, and your skin is quite pale, but you are extremely loud for a dead person. How did you get this into you head?” Ivan Ivanovic showed Arkady Semyonovic the death certificate. He let his eyes glide over the now crumpled paper with little attention, only to show with every possible body language which he was able to muster up, that he was in no way interested in Ivan Ivanovic’s shenanigans, and advised him to seek out a priest, since those, he reckoned, should be the ones in society most equipped at recognizing the dead. Ivan Ivanovic opened his eyes as if Arkady Semyonovic had pronounced an oracle with this answer; the solution to all Ivan Ivanovic’s woes. Without taking the death certificate back from Arkady Semyonovic’s hands, he hurried out the door, back into the cold, to look for a church.

This was definitely not an easy task for our friend, as he had abstained from any spiritual or religious matter with pious scrupulousness. Eventually, Ivan Ivanovic managed to find a priest, who stood pensively by a small chapel overlooking the frozen river, on the other side of town. “Venerable father!” he called to him from afar. Panting, he dropped to his knees at the priest’s feet, raised his head and asked in a pleading voice, “Am I dead!?” The priest, startled by the spectacle which was taking place before his eyes, gestured the best man to stand up. “Why speakest thou such strange language, dear sir?” “Everyone has declared me dead!” said Ivan Ivanovic, who at this point was able to hold back his tears only with difficulty. “And what drives them to say this? After all, you do not resemble any of the dead who frequent me with regularity.” “It is that doctor, that cursed doctor, who has declared me dead!” wiping the first tears of his cheek. “And which doctor was this, then, who drove you to such apparent despair?” “ Ignatij Ippolitovic!” by now not bothering to hold back the tears. “Ah… Doctor Filosofov? But that is a well-learned man, one who has spent many years at the academy. I’m afraid I must resign myself to his conclusions then. Surely my knowledge of the dead can in no way match the expertise of someone from the academy, right? Hmmm… I don’t believe we have any service planned for tomorrow yet. I’ll write up your burial for then. Right, then I will see you tomorrow, regular time, on the east side of the church. Oh, and above all, don’t forget to bring your dear and loved ones, surely they would not want to miss this saddest of occasions.” Ivan Ivanovic had stopped listening halfway through the priest’s story and had blankly looked at the earth beneath his feet with mortified eyes, wondering if all of this was actually happening, or whether he was merely stuck in some terrible dream inside his sickly head.

When he returned home the next morning, the vestibule and the hallway of the hut were completely filled with people, known and less known to Ivan Ivanovic, and whose noisy murmur had already surprised him from afar. When he, now, entered the vestibule the crowd fell silent instantly, and everyone present there turned their heads towards Ivan Ivanovic, looking at him intensely. Through the crowd a narrow path formed that led to Ivan Ivanovic’s room, the door of which was already open. Not knowing what else to do with the present situation, Ivan Ivanovic decided to trail the path to his room. He again went completely white when he saw that his bed had given way to a coffin, which was entirely decorated with burning candles, bouquets of flowers and frankincense. Petrified, he remained standing in the doorway before being, after some time, sent into the room by a firm punch in the back. “Lie down, Ivan Ivanovic!” sounded resoundingly a voice unknown to him. “It behooves a dead person to lay down in a coffin. After all, have you ever seen a dead person being buried standing up?” Ivan Ivanovic turned around, spread his arms and shouted loudly: “I’m not dead!” This outburst was met by an indignant and disapproving murmur from the crowd filling the corridor. After the murmuring had died down somewhat, a very imposing-looking, yet unknown to Ivan Ivanovic, man, stepped into the room and addressed him, without first introducing himself in the customary fashion:

“Are you trying to imply, Ivan Ivanovic, that doctor Ignatij Ippolitovic is a quack? Even though he spent many years at the academy? Or are you questioning the expertise of Karl Edmundovic, who is doing his work at the most renowned university of our country? For shame!” Upon hearing all this, Ivan Ivanovic lowered his head, sighed deeply, and, realizing there was no point in resisting any further, gingerly lay down in the coffin.
