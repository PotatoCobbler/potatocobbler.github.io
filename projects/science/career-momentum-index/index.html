A Citation-Based Career Momentum Index

A simple citation-weighted approach to describing whether a scientist's research impact is increasing, stable, or declining over the course of a career.

Introduction

Conventional bibliometric indicators such as total citations, the h-index, and the i10-index are useful for describing the accumulated impact of a scientist's work. However, these quantities are strongly dependent on factors such as career length, as well as the field in which one is active: some fields are larger than others, naturally resulting in more citations, and researchers with a long publication history will generally accumulate more citations than someone at an earlier stage of their career. Moreover these metrics cannot decrease over time, citations on a publication from long ago contributes as much as one published today.

The question addressed here is different: how is the scientific impact of an individual researcher evolving over time?

Rather than tracking the aforementioned measures over time, we here describe an internally relative metric which explicitely uses the age of the publication. A positive value value may indicate increasingly influential or promising research activity, a stable value may indicate sustained impact, whereas a declining value may indicate decreasing research momentum, reduced productivity, or the natural slowing of a career.

The Career Momentum Index

For a given evaluation year \(Y_i\), take the total citation count for a given publication \(n\) in the present year \(C_n\) and divide that number by that publications age (\(Y_i\) - \(Y_n\)). Do this for all publications with a publication year preceding the current year, i.e. (\(Y_i\) < \(Y_n\)), and sum everything up: this is the Career Momentum Index for that year \(A(Y_i)\).

\[ A(Y_i) = \sum_{Y_n < Y_i} \frac{C_n}{Y_i-Y_n} \]
\(A(Y_i)\) Career Momentum Index evaluated in year \(Y_i\).
\(C_n\) Total citation count of publication \(n\).
\(Y_i\) Year at which the index is evaluated.
\(Y_n\) Publication year of article \(n\).

The index \(A(Y_i)\) for a certain year is thus not fixed, and has to be recalculated each year.

Key principle: the index does not simply reward more recent publications, rather, it measures the impactfulness of ones entire scientific output throughout ones career: old publications start weighting less and less, unless they continue to receive citations.

In contrast to other career metrics, the index can therefore decrease over time.

From the index to career trajectory

More interesting than individual career momentum indices, is their change over time: the Career Momentum Gradient. Calculating \(A(Y_i)\) for successive years and calculate the trajectory by fitting a simple linear regression:

\[ A(Y_i)=\Gamma Y_i+b \]

where \(\Gamma\) is the slope of the Career Momentum Index with respect to time. The slope is obtained by ordinary least squares:

\[ \Gamma = \frac{ \sum_i (Y_i-\overline{Y}) (A(Y_i)-\overline{A}) }{ \sum_i (Y_i-\overline{Y})^2 } \]

The slope \(\Gamma\), is used as the principal descriptor as it directly measures the rate at which the Career Momentum Index changes with time.

\(\Gamma>0\) indicates increasing citation momentum.

\(\Gamma\approx0\) indicates relatively stable citation momentum.

\(\Gamma<0\) indicates declining citation momentum.

Thus, the index and its slope provide two complementary pieces of information: \(A(Y_i)\) describes the citation-weighted state of a career at a particular point in time, while \(\Gamma\) describes its direction of change.

Interpretation

The Career Momentum Gradient is deliberately an internal career metric: it does not change solely based on the absolute number of citations. As such, it can put a number to the stage of ones scientific career, for example:

Early-career growth

A researcher who begins producing highly cited work can show a rapid increase in \(A(Y)\). Because recent publications have a relatively small age denominator, successful new work can have a particularly strong effect on the index.

Sustained influence

An older publication does not necessarily become irrelevant. If it continues to accumulate citations, its increasing citation count can compensate for its increasing age. This allows the index to recognize long-lasting scientific influence.

Stagnation

If a researcher stops producing influential work and their existing publications cease to accumulate citations, the denominators of the existing contributions continue to increase. The overall index will consequently tend to decline.

Declining momentum

A sustained negative slope may indicate decreasing research activity or influence. However, it should not automatically be interpreted as a decline in scientific quality. Career breaks, changes of research field, changes in citation practices, and retirement can all produce similar trajectories.

Limitations

The Career Momentum Index is intentionally simple and should not be interpreted as a complete measure of scientific quality. Citation practices differ substantially between fields, papers may accumulate citations for reasons unrelated to scientific importance, and citation counts themselves are affected by publication type, collaboration size, and the size of the relevant research community.

The index is therefore most informative when applied longitudinally to the same researcher and interpreted alongside their publication record and research context. In particular, the gradient \(\Gamma\) should be understood as a measure of citation momentum, rather than as a direct measurement of scientific ability, productivity, or quality.

Lastly, the gradient as defined above covers the entire scientific career. It may in cases be more informative to evaluate it only for the past, say, 5 years \(\Gamma_5\). This would artificially neglect older publications, and thus evaluate only recent progress, for example during ones PhD or postdoc, etcetera.

Summary

The Career Momentum Index combines cumulative citation impact with publication age:

\[ A(Y_i) = \sum_{Y_n < Y_i} \frac{C_n}{Y_i-Y_n} \]

The resulting time series can then be summarized by its linear slope:

\[ A(Y_i)=\Gamma Y_i+b \]

The sign of \(\Gamma\) provides a simple description of the direction of the career trajectory: positive for increasing momentum, approximately zero for stable momentum, and negative for declining momentum.

The strength of the approach lies in its simplicity. Rather than attempting to assign an absolute numerical value to a scientist's importance, it asks a more focused question: is the citation-based impact of this scientist's work increasing, stable, or declining over the course of their career?
