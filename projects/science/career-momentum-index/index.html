A Citation-Based Career Momentum Index

A simple citation-weighted approach to describing whether a scientist's research impact is increasing, stable, or declining over the course of a career.

Introduction

Conventional bibliometric indicators such as total citations, the h-index, and the i10-index are useful for describing the accumulated impact of a scientist's work. However, these quantities are strongly dependent on career length. A researcher with a long publication history will generally accumulate more citations than someone at an earlier stage of their career.

The question addressed here is different: how is the scientific impact of an individual researcher evolving over time?

The aim is therefore not to produce an absolute ranking of scientists. Instead, the index is intended as an internal measure of career trajectory. A rising value may indicate an increasingly influential or promising research career, a stable value may indicate sustained impact, while a declining value may indicate decreasing research momentum, reduced productivity, or the natural slowing of a career.

The Career Momentum Index

For a given year \(Y_i\), each publication contributes its total citation count divided by its age. The Career Momentum Index is defined as

\( A(Y_i) = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N(Y_i)} \frac{C_n}{Y_i-Y_n} \)

where \(C_n\) is the total number of citations received by publication \(n\), \(Y_n\) is its year of publication, and \(N(Y_i)\) is the number of publications available before the year under consideration.

\(A(Y_i)\) Career Momentum Index evaluated in year \(Y_i\).
\(C_n\) Total citation count of publication \(n\).
\(Y_i\) Year at which the index is evaluated.
\(Y_n\) Publication year of article \(n\).

The central idea: recent influential work contributes strongly to the index, whereas older publications contribute less unless they have accumulated a correspondingly large number of citations.

Consequently, an old paper that continues to attract citations can remain an important contributor to the index. The metric therefore does not simply reward publication recency; it rewards the combination of citation impact and temporal persistence.

Why divide by publication age?

Citation counts accumulate over time. Without an age correction, a paper published twenty years ago has had substantially more opportunity to accumulate citations than a paper published two years ago.

The factor

\( \frac{1}{Y_i-Y_n} \)

introduces a simple temporal weighting. A recent paper with many citations therefore has a large contribution, while an old paper must demonstrate sustained citation activity to maintain a large contribution.

This also gives the index an interesting property: a publication does not permanently disappear from the measure simply because it becomes old. If an older paper continues to receive citations, its cumulative citation count can compensate for the increasing age denominator.

For example, if a paper published in 2016 receives a large number of additional citations in 2026, its contribution to the researcher's present-day index increases. This captures the idea that scientific influence can sometimes emerge or strengthen long after publication.

From the index to career trajectory

Calculating \(A(Y_i)\) for successive years produces a time series describing the evolution of the researcher's citation momentum.

The trajectory can then be summarized by fitting a linear regression,

\( A(Y_i) = mY_i+b \)

where \(m\) is the slope of the Career Momentum Index with respect to time. The slope is obtained by ordinary least squares:

\( m = \frac{ \displaystyle\sum_i (Y_i-\overline{Y}) \left[A(Y_i)-\overline{A}\right] }{ \displaystyle\sum_i (Y_i-\overline{Y})^2 } \)

The slope \(m\), rather than the Pearson correlation coefficient, is used as the principal descriptor because it directly measures the rate at which the career momentum index changes with time.

\(m > 0\) Increasing momentum
\(m \approx 0\) Stable momentum
\(m < 0\) Declining momentum

Thus, the index and its slope provide two complementary pieces of information: \(A(Y_i)\) describes the current citation-weighted state of a career, while \(m\) describes its direction of change.

Interpretation

The important quantity for characterizing the trajectory is the slope of the fitted line. A positive slope indicates increasing citation momentum, a slope close to zero indicates a relatively stable trajectory, and a negative slope indicates declining momentum.

This measure is deliberately an internal career metric. Its primary purpose is to examine how the citation impact of one scientist changes over time, rather than to establish an absolute ranking between scientists.

What the index captures

The index is designed to capture several different career trajectories that conventional cumulative citation counts can obscure.

Early-career growth

A researcher who begins producing highly cited work can show a rapid increase in \(A(Y)\). Because recent publications have a relatively small age denominator, successful new work can have a particularly strong effect on the index.

Sustained influence

An older publication does not necessarily become irrelevant. If it continues to accumulate citations, its increasing citation count can compensate for its increasing age. This allows the index to recognize long-lasting scientific influence.

Stagnation

If a researcher stops producing influential work and their existing publications cease to accumulate citations, the denominators of the existing contributions continue to increase. The overall index will consequently tend to decline.

Declining momentum

A sustained negative slope may indicate decreasing research activity or influence. However, it should not automatically be interpreted as a decline in scientific quality: career breaks, changes of research field, changes in citation practices, and retirement can all produce similar trajectories.

Limitations

The Career Momentum Index is intentionally simple and should not be interpreted as a complete measure of scientific quality. Citation practices differ substantially between fields, papers may accumulate citations for reasons unrelated to scientific importance, and citation counts themselves are affected by publication type, collaboration size, and the size of the relevant research community.

The index is therefore most informative when applied longitudinally to the same researcher and interpreted alongside their publication record and research context.

In particular, the slope \(m\) should be understood as a measure of citation momentum, rather than as a direct measurement of scientific ability, productivity, or quality.

Summary

The Career Momentum Index combines cumulative citation impact with publication age:

\( A(Y_i) = \displaystyle\sum_n \frac{C_n}{Y_i-Y_n} \)

The resulting time series can then be summarized by its linear slope:

\( A(Y_i)=mY_i+b \)

The sign of \(m\) provides a simple description of the direction of the career trajectory: positive for increasing momentum, approximately zero for stable momentum, and negative for declining momentum.

The strength of the approach lies in its simplicity. Rather than attempting to assign an absolute numerical value to a scientist's importance, it asks a more focused question: is the citation-based impact of this scientist's work increasing, stable, or declining over the course of their career?
